Research Thesis

In Defence of Democracy: The Worst Form of Government, Except for All the Others. Explaining the Russia–Ukraine War by the Belligerents' Domestic Regimes

Author: Roberto Sforza
Institutions: LUISS Guido Carli & Sciences Po Bordeaux
Year 2025

This research offers a liberal analysis of the Russia-Ukraine War, interpreted not merely as a geopolitical confrontation, but as the outcome of a fundamental asymmetry between regime types. Drawing on Interwar Idealism, Michael Doyle’s Liberal Democratic Peace Theory, and Andrew Moravcsik’s New Liberalism, the thesis argues that “the war transcends the mere matters of territorial reconfiguration or historical claims, constituting a collision between two states whose internal orders are shaped by diametrically opposed philosophies of governance.” The Russian full-scale invasion of Ukraine is read as a product of the personalist and autocratic structure of the Kremlin, where “power and policy preferences are effectively monopolized by Mr. Putin and his chosen circle of oligarchs and loyalists,” allowing imperial nostalgia to become state action. Conversely, Ukraine is portrayed as a quasi-democracy whose imperfect but resilient aspiration towards Europeanisation and liberal norms opened the door to international solidarity. The work denounces liberal democracy’s structural vulnerability to internal subversion through its own procedures, advancing a robust normative stance: “The right to vote cannot include the right to abolish the system that guarantees it […] Strip democracy of liberalism, and all that makes it worth defending will already have been lost. It will have become a loaded gun in the hands of the mob; better to seize it before it fires.” The concluding chapter calls for an assertive and vigilant liberalism – one capable of defending itself both from within and without – echoing the spirit of militant democracy: “Should democracy be swayed to betray its principles, to betray liberty, to strangle its parent, then it must be disciplined. For democracy can either be liberal, or be it cannot.” The dissertation is informed by Locke’s natural-rights Liberalism, Kant’s Republican Cosmopolitanism, Machiavelli’s cautions, and Schumpeter’s Theory of Democracy; it further employs Wilsonian Idealism together with the above-mentioned frameworks of Doyle and Moravcsik, along with other influential scholars, political scientists, and philosophers such as Van den Bergh, Zakaria, Gel'man, Maffettone, Sartori and more; it finally grounds its normative defence of constitutional self-preservation in Rawls’s Reasonableness and Overlapping Consensus, Popper’s Paradoxes of Tolerance and Democracy, and Loewenstein’s Militant Democracy doctrine
